   Content: ISA in plain language

- What it is and why it helps: ISA is a simple, consistent way to describe what was done in an experiment so others can understand, reuse, and reproduce it. It organizes project metadata at three levels and ties the design, methods, and results together with clear terms.

Key components and how they relate

- Investigation → Studies → Assays
  - Investigation: the project-wide context. It includes the project title, description, who did it, related publications, and any ontology terms that define the overall project.
  - Studies: the experimental units or setups within the project. Each study has its own design notes, protocols, materials, variables (factors), and can contain one or more assays.
  - Assays: the concrete measurements or observations made in a study. Each assay records what was measured, the method/technology, any data files produced, and links to the materials and processes used.

Common objects you’ll see (what they mean in plain terms)

- Protocols: standardized procedures or SOPs used in the study. They describe the steps you follow at a high level.
- Processes: the concrete sequence of steps applied in a protocol (the “how” inside the protocol).
- Materials: the things studied or used to run the experiment (samples, extracts, reagents). Often split into sources, samples, and other materials.
- Factors: the experimental variables that differ across conditions (e.g., genotype, treatment, time point). These define the design of a study.
- Data files: the outputs or results from assays (tables, images, spectra, etc.).
- Ontology annotations: standardized terms to describe concepts (like “measurement type” or “treatment”) with a controlled source and identifier (termSource and termAccession).
- Publications: the papers or reports that describe the work.
- People: those involved (authors, contacts, affiliations) and their roles.

A practical mental model

- Think of ISA as a labeled, linked set of notebooks:
  - An overarching project (Investigation) contains multiple studies.
  - Each study holds its design, what was used (materials), how it was done (protocols and processes), what was measured (assays), and any results (data files).
  - Ontology terms give everyone a common language for what was measured and how.
- The emphasis is on explicit connections: which protocol was used in which study, which materials were involved, which factors varied, and which data files came from which assays.

What to look for when reading life-science papers (quick checklist)

- Project context
  - Is there a clear project title and a short description?
  - Are publications and author/affiliation details listed?
  - Are ontology terms or a controlled vocabulary used to define the study’s scope?

- Study design
  - Are the studies described with their design variables (factors) and their levels?
  - Are the experimental conditions and treatments stated?

- Methods and materials
  - Are protocols or references to standard procedures named or cited?
  - Are materials described (samples, extracts, reagents) and their sources?
  - Are there explicit references to how materials were prepared or handled?

- Measurements and data
  - Are the assays described (measurement type and technology)?
  - Are data files or data repositories specified (where to access the results)?
  - Are units and any required annotations defined?

- Reproducibility and linking
  - Can you trace each data file back to a study, a protocol, and the materials used?
  - Are terms annotated with ontology sources and accession numbers?

- Quick sanity checks
  - Do the study descriptions, protocols, materials, and data align logically (no missing links)?
  - Are there any uncertainties about sample origin, processing steps, or data formats?

A simple takeaway

- ISA helps you see “what was done, how it was done, with what, and what was produced,” all tied together by standard terms. Use the three levels (Investigation, Studies, Assays) as your backbone, and look for clear links among design (factors), methods (protocols/processes), and results (data files) plus standardized terminology (ontology annotations).
