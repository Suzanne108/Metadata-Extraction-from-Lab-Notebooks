   Content: ISA in plain language

- What it is and why it helps
  - ISA is a simple framework for describing how a scientific investigation was done, so others can understand, reproduce, and reuse the data.
  - It organizes metadata at three levels, so you can see the big picture (the project) and the concrete steps (the measurements) all in one place.
  - Using a standard structure makes data more searchable, comparable across studies, and easier to publish and share.

- The three key components and how they relate
  - Investigation: the top-level container for a project. It holds the project title, description, who worked on it, related publications, and links to the individual studies.
  - Study: a coherent experimental unit inside the project. Each study has its own goal, design, and variables, and it can link to one or more assays. Think of a study as a self-contained chunk of experiments under the same project umbrella.
  - Assay: a specific measurement or data-generation activity within a study. An assay records what was measured, with which technology, using particular protocols, on particular materials, and what data files were produced.
  - In short: Investigation → Studies → Assays. A project contains studies; each study contains assays.

- Common objects you’ll see across levels
  - Protocols: documented steps or methods used to carry out procedures.
  - Processes: how a protocol is actually carried out on materials (the real-world steps that happen during the work).
  - Materials: the things used or produced (samples, reagents, equipment, cells, organisms).
  - Factors: experimental variables that are changed or tested (e.g., genotype, treatment, dose, time point) and the values chosen for each.
  - Data files: the results or raw data generated by assays (with file names, formats, and locations).
  - Ontology annotations: standardized terms tied to external vocabularies to describe concepts precisely (e.g., treatment names, tissue types). These come with references to the governing ontologies.

- A practical mental model
  - Imagine a tree: the root is the Investigation (the whole project context). The branches are Studies (each branch is a coherent experimental unit). Off each branch are twigs called Assays (each twig is a specific measurement or data-collection event). Each level carries its own details, and they link to protocols, materials, factors, and data files as applicable.

- Quick what to look for when reading life-science papers (ISA-oriented checklist)
  - Project context
    - The title and a clear description of the overall investigation.
    - Who the contributors are and their affiliations.
    - References to related publications.
  - Studies within the investigation
    - How many studies and what each one was trying to test or compare.
    - The experimental variables (factors) and their levels (e.g., different genotypes, doses, time points).
    - Any standardized protocols or references to protocols used.
    - What materials or samples were involved in each study.
  - Assays and data
    - The types of measurements or observations (assay types) and the technologies used.
    - The specific protocols and materials linked to each assay.
    - The data produced (data files, formats, storage locations or repositories).
  - Ontology and standard terms
    - Use of controlled vocabulary or ontology terms to describe factors, materials, and measurements.
    - Any ontology source references that show which vocabularies were used.
  - Links and accessibility
    - Clear connections between the investigation, its studies, and the assays.
    - Availability of data and publications (DOIs, accession numbers, URLs).
  - Dates and versioning
    - Submission date and any public release date, so you know the timeline and the version of metadata.

Notes on the ISA schema (how the structure looks in practice)
- The Investigation object includes fields like title, description, submissionDate, publicReleaseDate, and lists for ontologySourceReferences, publications, people, studies, and comments.
- The Investigation serves as the container; it references Studies (an array of study objects).
- Each Study has its own schema, and within a study you’ll find details about factors, protocols, materials, and the link to one or more assays.
- Across all levels, protocols describe standardized steps, processes show how those steps are applied, materials capture what was used, factors capture experimental variables, and data files store the results.
- Ontology annotations and ontologySourceReferences help ensure terms are consistent and searchable across studies.

If you want to dive deeper, you can check the official ISA schema starting with Investigation (to see which fields are commonly used and how nested objects are organized). The schema makes explicit which fields are at the top level and how Studies and Assays are nested under Investigation, but the practical takeaway is: use the three-level structure to map out project, experiments, and measurements, along with the protocols, materials, and data that accompany them.
