What ISA is and why it’s useful
- ISA stands for Investigation, Study, and Assay. It’s a practical way to capture what a project did, how it was done, and what data were produced.
- It helps you track context (why the work was done), experimental design (what variables were tested), and results (what was measured and where the data live).
- It emphasizes standard terms and clear linking between planning, execution, and data, so you can reproduce or reuse parts of the work later.

Key components and how they relate
- Investigation (top level)
  - The project umbrella. It contains the overall title, description, who worked on it, publications, and links to all Studies and data.
  - Think of it as the “story arc” of the whole project.
- Studies (inside an Investigation)
  - A Study is a coherent experimental unit or a set of related experiments.
  - A Study lists its design ideas (design descriptors), the protocols it used, the materials involved, any processing steps, and the Assays that came from it.
  - It also groups together researchers, publications, and any materials or data specific to that study.
- Assays (inside a Study)
  - An Assay is a concrete measurement or readout activity (e.g., a gene expression test, a metabolite assay, a imaging readout).
  - An Assay references the measurement type and technology, uses data files to capture results, and ties back to the materials and processes used to obtain those results.
- How they connect
  - Investigation contains Studies; each Study contains Protocols, Materials, Processes, Factors, and Assays; Assays produce Data Files.
  - Protocols describe the steps you follow; Materials describe what you used (samples, extracts, reagents); Factors capture the experimental conditions; Data Files store the results.

Common objects you’ll encounter
- Protocols
  - Standardized procedures or sets of steps used in experiments.
  - May include parameters and components (specific steps, reagents, or tools).
- Processes and process sequences
  - The actual workflow steps instantiated during the study.
- Materials
  - Includes Sources (where materials come from), Samples (the materials that were tested), and Other Materials (extracts, reagents, etc.).
- Factors
  - The experimental variables that change across samples or conditions (e.g., genotype, treatment, time point).
- Data files
  - The produced results, described by type (e.g., Raw Data File, Derived Data File, Image File, etc.). Data files point to the results of Assays.
- Ontology annotations
  - Controlled terms used to describe variables, protocols, or measurements. They include a term source (which ontology), a term accession, and the specific term value.
- Units and descriptors
  - Lists of defined units and characteristic categories help avoid repeating the same definitions for every material or assay value.

A practical mental model
- Imagine a tree: Investigation at the top, with one or more Studies beneath, and each Study producing one or more Assays.
- Each branch (Study or Assay) points to the specific Protocols, Materials, and Factors used.
- Data files are the leaves, recording the outcomes of the Assays.
- Ontology annotations provide standardized vocabulary so terms mean the same thing across projects.

What to look for in life-science papers (a quick checklist)
- Investigation-level context
  - Project title and purpose, high-level description, and main publications or datasets.
  - Contact people and affiliations; any ontology or vocabulary references mentioned.
- Studies
  - For each study: a clear objective or design descriptor; how samples were organized.
  - Protocols used: are there named protocols or a description of the steps? key parameters?
  - Materials: what samples or extracts were used; where did they come from?
  - Factors: what variables were tested (e.g., treatment vs control, time points, genotype)?
  - Associated data: what data files were generated (type and format), where the data are stored, and how they can be accessed.
- Assays
  - Measurement type and technology used to obtain results.
  - Data files: list of results produced and their types; any links to repositories.
  - Materials and processes: referenced materials and the steps used to generate data.
- Ontologies and standard terms
  - Any references to controlled vocabularies or ontology terms (and their sources/accessions).
- Linking and traceability
  - Clear connections from Investigation to Studies to Assays; explicit links between protocols, materials, and data.
  - Descriptions should enable someone else to understand what was done and potentially reproduce the workflow.

Tips for quickly extracting ISA-style info from papers
- Skim the abstract and intro for the Investigation’s scope; note the overall project aim.
- Look for a methods-like section that describes experimental design (Studies), what was tested (Factors), and what was measured (Assays).
- Identify any tables or figures that list materials and data types; check for data availability or repositories.
- Watch for mentions of standardized terms or ontologies (even if informal) to understand the vocabularies used.

If you want to validate structure during review
- The ISA schemas define required vs. optional fields and how nested objects are organized. You can map a paper’s sections to fields like title, description, factors, protocols, materials, assays, and data files to ensure a coherent ISA-like record.

In short: ISA helps you capture the full metadata story of a project, from planning through data, in a consistent, linked structure that makes it easier to understand, reproduce, or reuse the work.
