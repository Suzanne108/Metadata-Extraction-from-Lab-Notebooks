   Content: What ISA is and why it’s useful
- ISA stands for Investigation, Study, and Assay. It’s a simple, structured way to describe an experiment from project-level context down to individual measurements.
- It helps you capture what was done, with which materials, under which conditions, and what data was produced—so others can understand, reproduce, and reuse the work.
- The structure is designed to link a project (Investigation) to several concrete experiments (Studies), and to the actual measurements or tests (Assays) that come out of those studies.

Key components and how they relate
- Investigation → Studies → Assays: An Investigation is the top-level project frame. It contains one or more Studies, which in turn contain one or more Assays. Each level adds detail about design, materials, steps, and results.
- Studies collect: identifiers, titles, descriptions, study design descriptors (structured variables), protocols used, materials involved, processes, factors (experimental variables), and the assays that report the measurements.
- Assays capture: what was measured, how it was measured (measurement type and technology), the data outputs (data files), the materials used, and any procedures or process steps that produced the data.

Common objects you’ll see and what they carry
- Protocols: formal plans or step-by-step procedures used in the study. They may have a type, description, version, parameters, and listed components. Think of protocols as the recipe you follow during experiments.
- Processes and ProcessParameterValue: concrete, instantiated steps derived from protocols. They carry values for how a protocol was executed in a particular study (e.g., time, temperature, reagent amount).
- Materials (and their subtypes): describe the things you work with. Sources (starting materials), Samples (outputs of protocols that aren’t the special cases of Extract/Labeled Extract), and other Materials (like reagents or consumables). Materials can have characteristics (attributes) to describe properties.
- Factors and FactorValue: experimental variables (e.g., genotype, treatment, dose). A Factor defines the variable, and a FactorValue gives a specific condition for that variable in a study or assay.
- Data files: the outputs of measurements. DataFile objects have a type (e.g., Raw Data File, Derived Data File, Image File) and a place to store or reference the actual data.
- Ontology annotations and ontology sources: many terms are standardized using controlled vocabularies. OntologyAnnotation objects carry a term, often with a source reference (OntologySourceReference) so terms can be traced back to an official ontology.
- Sources, Samples, and MaterialAttributes: sources are starting biological materials; samples are outputs from protocols; material attributes describe descriptive properties of materials (e.g., characteristics and their values).
- Publications, People, and Organizations: track who did the work, where they’re affiliated, and how to cite or reference the study.
- Data relationships: a study links to protocols, materials, and processes; an assay links to data files and the materials it measured; ontology annotations provide standardized terms to ensure cross-study comparability.

Semantic Functions by Object
- Investigation: A project-level container that holds the overall title, description, submission date, related publications, people, ontology sources, and the list of studies included. It establishes the scope and provenance of the work.
- Study: A coherent experimental unit within an Investigation. It has an identifier, title, description, study design descriptors (ontology-annotated terms), protocols used, materials (sources, samples, other materials), a sequence of processes, assay definitions, and factors. It wires together the plan, inputs, and outputs.
- Assay: A specific measurement activity within a Study. It records what was measured (measurementType), how (technologyType), the data outputs (dataFiles), and the materials involved (samples and other materials). It ties the measurement to a concrete data result.
- Protocol: A defined plan for a set of steps. It lists protocolType, name, description, version, and parameters. Protocols are the source templates that drive Processes in Studies and Assays.
- ProtocolParameter: A parameter used by a Protocol to control a step (e.g., temperature, duration). It provides inputs to the protocol’s execution.
- Process: An instantiated step derived from a Protocol, showing how that protocol was carried out in a study. It can include parameter values and component steps that together form the execution path.
- ProcessParameterValue: The actual value used for a protocol parameter in a given process instance.
- Source: The starting biological material used in a Study. It can have characteristics to describe its properties.
- Sample: A material output from a protocol (not a Source or a special “Extract/Labeled Extract”). It represents a tangible result used in assays.
- Material: A general material object (not specifically a Source or a Sample). It can be used to describe things like extract names or labeled extracts, with attributes describing their characteristics.
- MaterialAttribute / MaterialAttributeValue: Characteristic categories and their specific values for materials. They help describe properties such as concentration, purity, or other descriptors.
- Factor / FactorValue: Experimental variables and their concrete values. For example, a FactorName might be “treatment” and a FactorValue might be “drug A, 10 μM.”
- Person / Organization: People and groups involved in the work, including affiliations and roles.
- Publication: The references to papers or reports describing the study, with fields like title, authors, and identifiers.
- OntologyAnnotation / OntologySourceReference: Standardized terms used to describe concepts. OntologySourceReference points to the ontology source; OntologyAnnotation carries the termValue or termAccession for precise, shareable meaning.
- DataFile: The actual data outputs produced by measurements. DataFile types describe what kind of data it is (raw, derived, image, etc.) and how it’s stored or accessed.

What to look for when reading typical life-science papers (a practical checklist)
- Project context: Look for a clear Investigation-level overview—title, description, and the people/organizations involved. Is there a public publication or DOI cited?
- Study design and scope: Check for study design descriptors that identify experimental variables and planned comparisons. Are factors defined, with their values described?
- Protocols and methods: Identify described protocols or references to standard protocols. Are the steps, parameters, and any instruments or platforms named?
- Materials and samples: Look for sources (starting materials), samples, and other materials used. Are material characteristics listed (concentrations, preparations, species, cell lines, etc.)?
- Processes and execution: See if there are explicit process sequences and parameter values showing how protocols were run in practice.
- Measurements and assays: Find the assays or measurement events. What was measured, with what technology, and what data were produced?
- Data outputs: Check for data files or repositories. Are raw and derived data described? Are there DOIs, file names, or links to data access?
- Ontology usage: Notice standardized terms and ontology references that describe concepts like measurement type, material properties, or experimental conditions.
- Reproducibility and provenance: Look for obvious links among Investigation, Studies, Protocols, and Assays that would allow someone else to reproduce the work, given access to the same materials and data.
- People and affiliations: Confirm who contributed and where they’re from, especially if you’re tracking collaborations or contacting authors.
- Documentation of limitations: See if the article notes any constraints or assumptions in study design or data interpretation.

Compact example to keep in mind
- An Investigation has multiple Studies. Each Study lists protocols used, materials (sources, samples), factors (like treatment), and a sequence of processes. Assays tied to a Study specify measurement types and data outputs. Protocols provide the steps; processes instantiate those steps with concrete parameter values; data files capture the results. Terms are often annotated with ontologies to ensure comparability across studies.
