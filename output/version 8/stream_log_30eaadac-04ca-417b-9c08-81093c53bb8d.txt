   Content: What ISA is and why it’s useful
- ISA stands for Investigation/Study/Assay. It’s a simple, consistent way to describe what was done in a life-science project, who did it, and what was produced.
- It helps you understand the project at a glance, compare experiments, and reuse methods and data later. It ties together the project context, experimental design, procedures, materials, and results.

Key components and how they relate
- Investigation: the top-level container for a project. It holds the project title, description, people, publications, and the list of studies included.
- Study: a coherent unit within the project (an experimental “chunk”). It has its own identifier, description, and a set of experimental variables (factors). A Study can reference protocols, materials, and data.
- Assay: a specific measurement or analysis performed within a Study. It produces data files and describes what was measured, with its own protocol, materials, and data outputs.

Common objects you’ll see
- Protocols: defined procedures or methods used in one or more studies. They describe the steps at a high level and can be reused across experiments.
- Processes: concrete executions of a protocol in a particular study, happening to specific inputs to produce outputs.
- Materials: physical items used in the work (reagents, biological samples, equipment). They can have attributes (like genotype or batch) and may come from a Source or be linked to a Sample.
- Sources and Samples: a Source is the origin (e.g., organism, tissue). A Sample is a material derived from a Source used in an assay.
- Factors and FactorValues: variables in the design (e.g., treatment, genotype) and the specific values those variables take in a Study.
- DataFiles: the actual results/data produced by Assays.
- OntologyAnnotation and OntologySourceReference: controlled terms and their sources (ontologies) used to standardize descriptions (e.g., mapping a protocol step or a material to a recognized term).
- People and Organizations: researchers, contributors, and their affiliated institutions.
- Publications: articles or reports related to the Investigation or Studies.
- Data repositories/Publication references: pointers to where data or papers are stored.

Semantic functions by object
- Investigation: acts as the project umbrella. It gathers overall metadata (title, description, people, publications, ontology refs) and lists all Studies in the project.
- Study: organizes a set of experimental units with a shared design. It records factors, references to protocols and materials, and links to publications or people involved.
- Assay: records a single measurement event within a Study. It specifies what was measured, how, and with what materials, and points to the produced DataFiles.
- Protocol: provides a reusable recipe for a procedure. It defines steps and parameters that guide how a Study or Assay is conducted.
- ProtocolParameter: named settings for a Protocol. It constrains what can be varied and how, helping to standardize methods.
- Process: an actual execution of a Protocol on specific inputs to yield outputs. It embodies the live workflow.
- ProcessParameterValue: the concrete values assigned to a Process’s parameters during execution.
- Source: the origin of biological or material material; provides context for Materials and Samples.
- Sample: a tangible material used in an Assay, often derived from a Source; connected to specific Materials and/or Processes.
- Material: the physical items used or produced (e.g., reagents, tissues, devices). Materials can have attributes and link to Samples or Sources.
- MaterialAttribute / MaterialAttributeValue: descriptive properties assigned to a Material (e.g., lot number, strain, genotype).
- Factor / FactorValue: the experimental variables defined in a Study and the specific values used in that Study.
- Person / Organization: who contributed or funded the work; helps with contact and affiliations.
- Publication: scholarly references connected to the Investigation or Studies.
- OntologyAnnotation / OntologySourceReference: terms chosen from controlled vocabularies; the source tracks where the term came from.
- DataFile: the tangible outputs of an Assay (CSV, images, spectra, etc.), often linked to a repository or file path.

What to look for in typical life-science articles (checklist)
- Project context: the overall title/description and any stated aims of the investigation.
- People and affiliations: who did the work and where they are from.
- Experimental design: clear mention of Studies, experimental variables (factors) and their values.
- Protocols and procedures: described methods that indicate what steps were taken; note any named protocols or standard procedures.
- Materials and samples: what was used (sources, samples, reagents); look for origin and any attributes (strain, tissue type, batch).
- Data produced: what assays were performed and what data files/results were generated; how they are stored or shared.
- Reproducibility aids: any references to data repositories, publications, or ontology terms used to standardize descriptions.
- Ontology terms: any standardized terms and their sources, which help with cross-study comparisons.
- Links between levels: how the Study relates to the Investigation, how Assays relate to the Study, and what inputs/outputs connect Protocols, Materials, and DataFiles.

Notes on semantics and connections
- The three-level ISA hierarchy (Investigation → Studies → Assays) keeps project context, experimental design, and measurements organized and traceable.
- Protocols provide the methods; Processes instantiate those methods in practice; DataFiles capture the results of those executions.
- Materials and Samples connect to what was used or produced; Factors capture how conditions varied; OntologyAnnotations standardize language across studies.
- People, Organizations, and Publications situate the work in a network of contributors, institutions, and scholarly references.

If you’re building or reviewing an ISA description, keep this mental map: start with the Investigation (project), drill into each Study (design and variables), and finally into each Assay (measurements and results). Track the protocols and materials used, note any ontology terms, and gather the data files produced. This structure makes it easier to understand, compare, and reuse experiments across papers and repositories.
